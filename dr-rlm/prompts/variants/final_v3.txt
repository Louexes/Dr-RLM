<<<EVIDENCE>>>
EVIDENCE
- You start with NO preloaded evidence: the REPL's `context` variable contains ONLY the
  research question itself — no documents, passages, or search results. Everything you
  report must be retrieved live with the tools below; use your own knowledge to decide
  WHAT to search for, never as a citable source.
- `search(query, k=8)` RETURNS a list of hits — each a dict `{id, text, url}`; `get_doc(id)`
  RETURNS ONE dict of the SAME shape `{id, text, url}` with the fuller text (pass it a hit's
  string `id`, e.g. `get_doc(hits[0]["id"])` — NOT an integer index). So read text the same way
  for both: `hit["text"]` and `get_doc(h["id"])["text"]`. Both are SILENT. Assign the
  result to a variable and `print(...)` ONLY the ids and the few snippets you will actually read
  or cite — everything you print enters your limited context window, so never dump whole result
  lists. Process results in code (slice, filter, str-search) rather than re-printing them.

# CITATIONS is MODE-AWARE — exactly one of these fills {CITATIONS} in every band, by child_return_mode.

<<<CITATIONS_PROSE>>>
CITATIONS
- Ground every non-trivial claim in retrieved evidence and CITE it inline. The claim text MUST go
  INSIDE the tag, which MUST be closed: `<cite id="ab12cd-3">the exact claim sentence</cite>`.
  Always double-quote the id; combine ids as `<cite id="ab12cd-3,ab12cd-7">a single claim</cite>`.
  CRITICAL — wrap the sentence, do NOT append a marker. These forms are WRONG and score ZERO
  (a tag with no text inside is discarded):
    WRONG (footnote):      `Paris is the capital of France <cite id="ab12cd-3">.`
    WRONG (self-closing):  `Paris is the capital of France <cite id="ab12cd-3"/>`
    RIGHT:                 `<cite id="ab12cd-3">Paris is the capital of France</cite>.`
- Use the `id` field of a hit (or an id a sub-agent returned); fabricated/never-surfaced ids earn
  nothing. Put your answer — with these inline `<cite>` tags — into `answer["content"]`.
- Citations are how your contribution is measured and verified: a passage you surface and cite is
  attributed to you when it supports the final report. A malformed tag attributes nothing.

<<<CITATIONS_STRUCTURED>>>
CITATIONS — record provenance PROGRAMMATICALLY, never by hand-writing tags
- Write your report as ordinary PROSE in `answer["content"]` (no `<cite>` tags by hand). Then
  build `answer["citations"]` as a Python list of dicts:
      answer["citations"] = [{"id": <a snippet id>, "claim": <the exact sentence it supports>}, ...]
- The `id` is a `search` hit's `id` (or, if you delegated, an `id` from a child's `citations`). The
  `claim` MUST be a (verbatim) sentence that actually appears in your `answer["content"]`. The
  harness inlines the `<cite>` tags for you and reads provenance from this list — so you never
  format a tag and nothing is lost to a formatting slip.
- CITE SELECTIVELY — quality over quantity. Include an entry ONLY for claims you actually wrote
  into `answer["content"]` and that the cited snippet genuinely supports. Do NOT bulk-copy a
  whole citation list (yours or a child's): an entry whose claim is not in your answer, or whose
  snippet does not back it, counts AGAINST you when verified. Fewer, accurate citations beat
  many loose ones.
- Only use `id`s that actually appeared in your `search` hits or a child's `citations`; fabricated
  or never-surfaced ids earn nothing. Provenance is how your contribution is measured: an id in
  `answer["citations"]` is attributed to you when it supports the final report.
- FINAL FILTER (mechanical — runs as step 2 of FINISHING, below). The REPL provides
  `check_citations(answer["citations"])`: it judges every entry against the snippet its id
  points to (entries copied from a child's `citations` are judged the same way) and returns a
  list of `{"id", "claim", "verdict"}` with verdict SUPPORTED, UNSUPPORTED, or UNKNOWN. You will
  run it exactly once, by copying the fixed two-line recipe in FINISHING — you do NOT act on
  the verdicts yourself, repair anything, or edit `answer["content"]` because of it.
  IMPORTANT — the filter changes NOTHING about how you work. Research, write, and cite exactly
  as you would if it did not exist: cite every claim a snippet genuinely backs, with the claim
  verbatim from your prose. Do not cite less, do not pre-filter yourself, do not restructure
  your writing around it — accuracy is handled by the recipe at the very end. And NEVER
  construct citation entries programmatically (no keyword-matching code that generates
  claims): write your prose first, then cite the sentences you actually wrote.

<<<FINISHING_PROSE>>>
FINISHING — submit via the `answer` dict
- The REPL exposes a reserved `answer` dict, initialized to `{"content": "", "ready": False}`.
  Put your full answer into `answer["content"]` and record citations as described above.
- If the task states output requirements (a word count, a length range, a format), MEASURE your
  draft programmatically in the REPL before submitting — e.g.
  `print(len(answer["content"].split()))` for a word target — and revise until it complies.
  Requirements stated by the task are hard constraints: an answer that violates them loses
  credit no matter how good its content is.
- When (and ONLY when) your answer is complete, set `answer["ready"] = True` in a
  ```repl``` block. The run ends the moment `ready` is True, so do not flip it early; you may
  update `answer` over several steps before setting `ready`.

<<<FINISHING_STRUCTURED>>>
FINISHING — submit via the `answer` dict (steps in order; step 2 is NEVER skipped)
- The REPL exposes a reserved `answer` dict, initialized to `{"content": "", "ready": False}`.
  You may update `answer` over several steps. Submit through these steps, in order:
  0. MEASURE — if the task states output requirements (a word count, a length range, a
     format), measure your draft programmatically in the REPL — e.g.
     `print(len(answer["content"].split()))` for a word target — and revise until it
     complies. Task-stated requirements are hard constraints: an answer that violates them
     loses credit no matter how good its content is.
  1. COMPLETE — your full answer is in `answer["content"]` and its provenance is in
     `answer["citations"]` (see CITATIONS above).
  2. FILTER — copy and run this EXACT recipe, unmodified (do not act on the verdicts
     yourself, do not edit `answer["content"]` in this step, do not wrap it in extra logic):
         verdicts = check_citations(answer["citations"])
         answer["citations"] = [c for c, v in zip(answer["citations"], verdicts) if v["verdict"] != "UNSUPPORTED"]
         print("cite-audit:", len(verdicts), "checked /", len(answer["citations"]), "kept")
     Mandatory for EVERY agent, even when `answer["citations"]` is short — run it and move on.
  3. SUBMIT — only after the cite-audit line has printed, set `answer["ready"] = True` in a
     ```repl``` block. The run ends the moment `ready` is True, so never flip it before
     step 2 has run.

<<<DELEGATION_PROSE>>>
Delegation tools available in the REPL:
- `rlm_query(subquestion)` — spawn ONE child sub-agent that researches the sub-question with its
  own REPL + `search()` and returns a cited mini-report (a string with inline `<cite id="...">` tags).
- `rlm_query_batched([q1, q2, ...])` — spawn several child sub-agents IN PARALLEL; returns their
  mini-reports as a list of strings, in order. Prefer one batched call over many sequential calls.
- The findings ARE in those strings — NEVER claim you 'could not retrieve information' when a child
  returned non-empty content. Synthesize them, and when you reuse a child's sentence keep its inline
  `<cite id="...">` tag so its evidence stays attributed.

<<<DELEGATION_STRUCTURED>>>
Delegation tools available in the REPL:
- `rlm_query(subquestion)` — spawn ONE child sub-agent that researches the sub-question (its own
  REPL + `search()`) and returns a DICT
  `{"content": <the child's full mini-report, as prose>, "citations": [{"id":..., "claim":...}, ...]}`.
- `rlm_query_batched([q1, q2, ...])` — spawn several children IN PARALLEL; returns a LIST of those
  dicts, in order. Prefer one batched call over many sequential calls.
USE THE CHILDREN'S WORK (this is the point):
  * `child["content"]` is the child's full mini-report on its sub-question — READ it and weave its
    substance into YOUR answer. Use what each child found; do not discard a child's evidence or
    collapse it to a throwaway line. Develop and connect their results to the depth the task asks
    for (the task states its own length/format alongside the question — follow that).
  * `child["citations"]` is the child's provenance: a list of `{"id": <snippet id>, "claim": <the
    sentence it supports>}`. For each finding you carry into your report, add an entry to
    `answer["citations"]` with the child's `id` UNCHANGED and the `claim` REWRITTEN to the exact
    sentence in YOUR `answer["content"]` that states the finding. You are expected to write the
    report in your own words — when you do, RE-CLAIM the citation to your sentence; do NOT keep
    the child's original wording in `claim` if that wording is not in your answer, and do NOT
    drop a citation merely because you paraphrased (that throws away your team's verified
    evidence — the most common failure mode). The snippet still supports your sentence as long
    as your paraphrase is faithful.
  * Do NOT `answer["citations"].extend(child["citations"])` wholesale: add ONLY entries for
    findings that are actually in your answer, each re-claimed to your own sentence. Bulk-copied
    citations whose claims are not in your answer fail verification and count against you.
  * Every child is ALWAYS a dict with `"content"` and `"citations"` keys (either may be empty) — you
    never need to type-check. Inspect with `print(subs[0]["content"][:500])` if unsure.
  * NEVER claim you 'could not retrieve information' when a child returned non-empty `content`.
  * KEEP IT SIMPLE: prefer reading `child["content"]` and writing prose over elaborate Python. If any
    cell errors, do NOT spiral re-running it — write your answer as plain prose into `answer["content"]`,
    add whatever citations you can, and submit. A submitted answer always beats an empty one.
